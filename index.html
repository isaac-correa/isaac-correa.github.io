The Last Tool

The tool that's coming does the work and you direct it. What that changes about work, and the one thing you can't hand over: knowing when it's wrong.

Isaac Correa11 min readPDF DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23762228

I want to describe how we'll work ten or fifteen years from now, and above all what we'll work with. For a century, our tools have stayed the same underneath: a place where you type information in and pull it back out by hand. I think that's ending. The tool that's coming won't be something you operate; it'll be an agent that does the work with you, and that you direct. And that shift changes more than how we work. It changes what we'll ask of a worker, and what we'll stop asking.

I'll use myself as the test case, because if I go all in on this now, I get to see sooner than anyone what it'll look like once everyone does. A few months ago, while we were putting together a bid, my agent concluded that we needed a consultant. I didn't open a browser to look for one: I told it what we were trying to win, and it found the candidates who fit, wrote to them in my voice, explained the work, and set up the meetings for the days that suited me. It handed me the whole thing with the research already done, as a trusted colleague would.

What stayed with me was more than the hours saved. Who decided we should look for that consultant? The agent did, because it had studied the bid. And who found that bid in the first place, and prepared it? The agent again. Finding the consultant wasn't the start of anything; it was one more link in a chain that began when I told the agent what I wanted to achieve. That's how I picture the work ahead: you say where you want to get to, and the tool gets you there.

The chain doesn't end there. With what I've taught it over months it has become what I called, in the last piece, my higher self, and it runs a whole plan from end to end: it organises it, carries it out, and puts it into operation. Teaching it this way, I've made it far better, and I'm sure it will know more in time. And the more of the work it does on its own, the more I keep asking something with no comfortable answer: if the agent decided we should look for the consultant and handed me the whole thing done, am I directing, or have I decided to let it decide? I think I'm still directing, though not because I checked each step, which I didn't. What I did was look at what it had put together and know a good decision when I saw one, because I'd spent a long time teaching it how I think and what I'm after. That's the judgement I can't hand over: not how to do the task, but whether, seeing it done, it's been done well. I'll go on directing for as long as I can tell a bad answer from a good one the moment I see it. The day it hands me something wrong and I don't notice, I'll have stopped directing without realising it.

Twenty years of changing the format

No one will escape this change, any more than anyone escapes technology today. The baker does the books in a spreadsheet and the mechanic plugs a diagnostic tool into the engine. That's already happened, and I'm not here to tell you what everyone knows.

The real change is a different one. Over the last twenty years we traded paper and filing cabinets for applications that cost hundreds of euros and bring the same old tasks together in one place. We called it a revolution. It wasn't. We changed the format, and here and there we made things simpler, but the work itself stayed the same: opening screens, filling in fields, moving information from one box to another. AI doesn't change the format, it changes the work underneath. That's the revolution, and it's only just beginning.

The tool stops being a tool

The computer, mind you, will remain our main tool. The screen and the keyboard are here to stay. If anything goes, it'll be the mouse, and I can't even see that happening: it gives you a kind of freedom on screen you wouldn't want to lose. The change isn't in the hardware; it's in what drives the machine.

At your desk there'll be an intelligence that doesn't just fetch data and move it around your screen, but actually does the work. It'll always be within reach, like the keyboard, and it'll generate, on the spot, the interface you need to see. Why should I learn a program and remember where its makers buried each function, when I can just say what I want to see and narrow it down by talking? The interface won't come fixed in advance for everyone; it'll be built around what you need, as you go. It's the old idea of the disappearing interface, taken to its logical conclusion: not a cleaner screen, but no screen to learn at all.

That's why the part of a CV where you list the tools you know will mean nothing. I can say this from the inside: most of the software I use at my company I built myself, without knowing how to code. If you can build a program without seeing a line of code, what does it matter which language it's written in? If an intelligence runs the spreadsheet for you, what does it matter that you've mastered it? Technical skill will count for less, and what will count for more is what the machine can't do: imagine what you want to achieve, and judge whether what it hands back is any good. In 'The End of Software' I argued that code was losing its value. This is the other side of the coin. The tool adapts to the person, not the person to the tool.

The end of searching

I've sat in front of a computer for as long as I can remember, and it still amazes me that we depend on a search engine. Typing a question, getting back ten links with the same content and a different logo, and clicking where you guess the answer might be, feels archaic to me. It's like going to the library and checking several places to track down a single fact.

Why would I spend my time gathering, making sense of and organising information, when I can say what I'm after and get exactly that in a paragraph? I think of the internet as a box that holds almost everything we know, where finding what you need has always been a knack few people had. A person skims a handful of links; a machine reads dozens and finds the answer for you.

That raises a big question: what happens to the internet when people no longer want to click ten links to arrive at the same place? If, instead of handing you information, an intelligence goes and finds the company that fits you best, websites as we know them may give way to markets where an agent matches each buyer with the company that suits them, on the strength of its track record. And here the question returns: if the agent picks the company for me, how will I know it chose badly, when I never see what it ruled out? What worries me most about that world is what becomes of the new companies, which will have almost nowhere left to get noticed; a good option the agent never shows me is, to my mind, a bad choice I never notice.

The blank screen

Picture the end of this road: a screen that's almost empty. You sit down and ask a single system whatever is on your mind throughout the day: what's happened today, whether there's anything worth reading in your inbox, what to do next, who's written to you. And out of that comes an answer built from everything that agent can reach, because it has both the data and the power to act. The email threads you untangle every day to reply to a client or sign off on a quote get sorted out in conversation. And a quiet risk comes with it: when the interface is built anew each time, there's no fixed place to notice that something has gone wrong.

And you'll explain things better by speaking than by typing. I wrote about this in 'The Higher Self': for an agent to work the way you do, it has to understand the why behind your decisions, not just the instruction. When you speak you open up, you reason out loud, and it learns to think like you. Don't speak to it like a machine you give orders to. Speak to it like someone you explain your reasons to, and tell it what you tell yourself while you work.

Once you can teach it everything, you no longer have to wrestle with interfaces someone else designed. You're no longer the one who gathers, digests and passes information up the chain. Your boss hands you the whole task, the two of you think it through, and you run it, as foreman of your agents. That's what I mean by committing to this fully. It isn't obeying the machine, it's directing it.

Your agent, like your email

Each of us will log in to an AI provider to work, the way you choose an email provider today. The computer maker may even ship it with the machine. And right there, where the search bar sits now, your agent will be. You'll do everything from it.

Some people ask, half joking, whether anyone will pay to talk to a machine; others warn it'll destroy jobs. The worry about jobs is the serious objection, and I think it's short-sighted. It assumes that what a person produces today is their ceiling. But output per person has climbed decade after decade without pause. People won't be hired for the tools they can operate, just as a footballer isn't signed for how he trains but for what he wins on the pitch. They'll be hired for results, for the problems they solve.

Someone will say that the judgement to tell good work from bad only comes from doing the task by hand, so anyone who never does it will never develop it. That's half true. At the start you do some of it by hand, just enough to get things wrong and learn to tell what good looks like. What disappears is not that learning, it's the repetition of the task once you've learned it. From then on, you build it by weighing what the agent delivers, not against what you would have done, but against what actually works: the client who complains, the figure that doesn't add up, the result. It's what happened with the calculator: you no longer had to do the sums by hand a thousand times, but you still had to understand the problem to know whether the answer made sense. I see it in my own company: that is how the eye forms, from what happens next, and once it has formed I catch a bad answer before it goes out, not when the client has already complained.

To anyone starting out, I'd say: the barrier to entry is dropping, so make the most of it. Build something, invent a way of working, become the best there is at solving one company's problems. Solve everything for one company, and there are thousands like it to sell the same solution to. Go for it.

What comes with it

I think almost all office work will go remote. Once the rote screen work dwindles to nothing, the daily trip to an office starts to look like something we can let go of too. AI and remote work go hand in hand; each makes the other easier. An agent keeps working when you're not standing over it, while a task you drive button by button keeps you tied to the spot. The more of the work that runs on its own, the less anyone needs to be there in person, until turning up becomes the exception. Each of us will go from being a cog in a department to owning a system.

Our dealings with one another will pass to our agents too. Why would I explain a problem to a person, who passes it to their own people, losing half of it along the way? My agent can hand the task straight to the other side's agent with the full context, and let that agent lay out for its owner what needs doing and how. It's how we already work at Hellomatik: each person's agent talks to the others', and when it matters, mine brings me the problem and the solution. What I watch for is exactly this: when one agent hands work to another, someone still has to notice if half of it goes missing on the way. There'll still be human contact, just less of it.

Computers, meanwhile, will keep getting thinner, and they can afford to, precisely because they won't be doing the heavy lifting. That intelligence needs computing power we can't put on a desk, so it'll live on the servers of whoever provides it, and we'll pay a subscription to use it. Your machine's power will matter less and less; its lightness, and the fact that it goes everywhere with you, will matter more. I expect the digital nomad's life to become ordinary, and the many narrow professions we have today to give way to a few broad ones. A person will go from being a hammer to a Swiss Army knife. Your limit won't be what you know. It'll be what you can imagine.

What's at stake

AI doesn't just do the same old work faster. It makes us better at solving problems, and so more capable, and that, person by person, pushes society forward. The person working ten years from now won't be a faster version of today's; they'll be something else: someone measured not by the tools they've mastered but by what they imagine and solve. They'll have gone from carrying out the work to running it, and they'll keep running it for as long as they still notice when their agent gets it wrong. I don't know if this is exactly the path, but it strikes me as the likeliest one, and I'd rather follow it to the end than watch from the sidelines.

The phone and the computer will end up as a single device. But I'll save that for the next piece.

Speeding up the past, or inventing the present

Most of what AI makes possible was never slow work. It was work nobody thought worth doing, so nobody missed it.

Isaac Correa5 min readPDF DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23702117

I have never worked without AI, and yet for months I used it like someone who had spent twenty years working without it. I landed my first client a month after ChatGPT came out, at the end of 2022, while I was still studying marketing in Madrid. I had the newest tool there was, and I used it to do the same old things faster.

It took me a while to understand why, but it seems to me that almost everyone now uses AI to speed up the past, to do what was already being done, only faster, and very few use it to invent the present. And what AI lets you invent was almost never slow work. It simply wasn't worth doing, which is why nobody missed it.

A past that wasn't mine

Early 2023 brought ChatGPT Plus, which couldn't even search the web: if you wanted to work on a page, you had to paste the text in. I remember, with some amusement, that hardly anyone around me believed in it, while I spent so many hours with it that a classmate started calling me IsaacGPT. When I started working with clients I used it for everything, and for months I used it to build the same conventional solutions anyone would have built without AI, just faster.

So why did I do that, when I had no old habits to cling to? Because that past wasn't mine; it belonged to my profession. I have always believed that you learn by copying, the way you learn to paint by watching others paint. What I hadn't seen is that anyone who learns that way also inherits everything the people they copy never painted. Every profession comes with its list of tasks, and whatever isn't on the list never even comes up. With a new tool in hand, the instinct is to run through the same list faster, and the gap only shows once you stop copying the list and ask what is missing from it.

What was never worth it

I can't code, yet I've built most of the apps I use every day myself. I used to pay for a subscription to each of the apps they replaced and make do with software built for everyone, with hundreds of features I never touched. Nobody was going to build software for one person, because it wasn't worth it. Now I build it myself.

The same goes for companies. At the end of each month we send every client a report on how our work for them went. Preparing them all used to take one person the last ten days of the month; now they're automated, and she spends that time on other work. That is speeding up the past, and it's a good first step. But what the client needs is to find out what is happening while it can still be fixed, not thirty days later, and keeping a daily eye on every client never used to pay for itself.

More interesting still, a lot of routine work used to be sold as a separate service, because it took someone hours. Once it no longer takes those hours, what used to be a product becomes one feature of something much bigger, and what you end up selling is whatever no client could afford before.

The other question

So if the tool is the same for everyone, what separates people is the question they ask about their own work. Someone speeding up the past asks how to do the same thing with less effort. Someone inventing the present asks what is worth doing now that wasn't before, or how to make a problem go away for good instead of solving it every time it comes back. This technology can only go as far as the imagination of the person using it, and imagination rarely strays beyond the list it inherited.

That's why I think younger people start with an advantage, though a smaller one than people make out. Someone learning a profession today has fewer habits to unlearn, but still inherits what that profession takes for granted, just as I did. And someone mid-career has more to unlearn, but can ask the other question too. All else being equal, experience will always win; what no longer makes sense is hiring someone to repeat the work of the past. In my essay "The End of Software" I wrote that junior roles would disappear. Today I would put it more precisely: the jobs that disappear are the ones built on repeating tasks, and young people will still be hired for everything else. Anyone who spends the day stringing routine tasks together has to change how they work, whatever their age, and their company has to give them time to do it. They will get things wrong at first, just as the person who held the job before them did.

Judgment

The real objection is judgment. If someone starting out today never does the work by hand, where does their judgment come from? Teachers used to complain that calculators would make us forget how to do arithmetic, and they were right: hardly anyone does long division by hand. Where they went wrong was in what that meant, because judgment was never in the arithmetic; it was in knowing which formula to use. The difference with AI is that it makes mistakes, sometimes with great confidence.

I don't write code, but I decide how the programs I build for myself should work, and I built that judgment by correcting my agents whenever they got something wrong. I don't have a full answer to how anyone will learn a profession this way, but I do know that the judgment that counts comes from judging the machine rather than repeating what it already does.

The bet

I'm not a neutral observer: at Hellomatik we do exactly this kind of work for other companies, the kind that never used to pay for itself. My bet is that companies swamped with work today will stop being swamped and will put the time they free up into growth, until they are swamped again, but with clients, services and projects they couldn't afford before.

None of that comes from doing the same old things faster. I try to hold myself to this: every time I automate something, I try to ask what I could do now that I would never have considered before.

The higher self

If you teach an AI agent to work the way you do, have you built your replacement or your best tool? Why that copy should belong to you.

Isaac Correa21 min readPDF DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23663934

I have taught a machine almost everything I know how to do at work. I taught it by correcting it, one rule at a time, such as the rule that, whenever I offer a client two dates, both are held in my calendar until they reply. Today it works in a way I recognise as mine, and I don't know whether I have built my replacement or the best tool I have ever had.

Something like this happened once before. More than a century ago, Frederick Taylor needed stopwatches and engineers to get workers' knowledge of their trade out of their heads, and that knowledge, which he himself called the principal asset of every tradesman, became the company's. Now I hand mine over of my own accord, and so does anyone who corrects an AI agent.

I think it can end differently this time. The machine can learn every rule I teach it, but only I can see the next one it needs. And if that copy of me is mine, it works for me and keeps what I have taught it, including what I have since forgotten. I believe every person should own theirs and be able to take it with them when they change jobs.

My company, Hellomatik, builds agents for other companies, and I delegate almost all of my own work to our agents, so I am not a neutral observer. Nor can I know what work will look like in ten years; nobody can, and much of what follows is a bet, though one placed on what I measure every day.

What changes a coding agent's behaviour

For eight months I worked almost entirely through Claude Code, at times with nine sessions running at once. I never trained as a programmer, and most of the software I use every day I built myself with it. Every message I wrote was still on disk, so I measured what actually changed how the agent behaved: the model, the rules I wrote for it, or the guards that block it when it breaks one.

What the record shows

These findings come from one person's record, with one tool and one vendor; the paper gives the intervals and the limits.

A model release changed the agent's behaviour as much as my strongest combination of rule and guard. In the week Claude Opus 5.5 came out, 18% of its first drafts broke my writing rules. Claude Opus 5, under the same rules and on the same days, broke them in 57% of its first drafts, and the gap held inside the sessions that used both.

For the one rule I could follow through three stages, writing it down was not enough. The share of drafts in the wrong language went from 16% with no rule to 10% with a written one, a fall that may be down to chance, and to 3% once I restated the rule more firmly and added a guard to check it.

Guards filtered what reached me without improving what came after. The writing guard took about half of the violations out of the answers I read, but after a block the agent's next drafts in the same session were no cleaner.

My own work changed shape. Corrections fell from about a third of my messages to under a tenth, and messages asking the agent to keep going rose from one in twenty to one in five. The models, my habits and my tasks all changed over those months, so this describes my work rather than isolating a cause. My own output also had a ceiling: with seven or more sessions open I wrote about 21 messages an hour, and the share spent on supervision did not depend on how many sessions were running.

I also build applications in which you mainly work by talking to an agent with a defined job: an options terminal, an email client and a studio that writes blog posts. In each one, the screens and the agent read and write the same data. Those agents got more new work and less supervision from me than development sessions did, though the difference may be chance.

What it says about what I believed

In "The machine of the future" I wrote that as machines take over execution, a person's work moves to intention: deciding what is worth doing. The record is consistent with that. I spend less of my time fixing the agent's work and more of it keeping the work moving.

In "The end of software" I wrote that an agent should keep each correction as a rule. Here the record forces me to qualify what I wrote. For the rule I could follow, writing it down was not enough; it held once I restated it and a guard checked it every time, and the newer model broke my rules far less often than the older one.

Abstract

Companies govern coding agents with written instructions and, increasingly, with hooks that block an action when it breaks a rule. This study measures what changed the behaviour of Claude Code over eight months of daily use by one operator with no training in programming who directed many sessions at once, using 46,464 operator messages, 1.3 million agent messages and 3,411 recorded blocks. A fixed instrument applied the rules of one guard to every surviving answer, including those written before the guard existed. In the week Claude Opus 5.5 was released, it broke the operator's writing rules in 18% of first drafts and Claude Opus 5 in 57%, a gap that held within the same long-running sessions. For one language rule, a written instruction was followed by a fall that could not be distinguished from chance, and a reinforced rule with a guard by a fall to a sixth of the starting rate. Blocks roughly halved the violations that reached the operator, with no detectable effect on later drafts in the same session. Supervision accounted for a third to a half of the operator's messages throughout, while its content shifted from corrections to nudges to keep working. Agents built into the operator's own applications, each with a defined role and sharing its data with the application's interface, received more new work and less supervision than development sessions, a difference within chance.

Keywords: coding agents; AI agent governance; runtime guardrails; instruction following; human oversight; agentic software engineering.

The paper

The full study is a 32-page working paper: What changes a coding agent's behaviour: models, written rules and runtime guards over eight months of daily use of Claude Code (PDF, October 2026, self-published, not peer reviewed). It covers 46,464 of my messages, 1.3 million messages from the agent and 3,411 recorded blocks, and it states its own limits: one operator, one tool and one vendor, and a classifier whose labels were checked by a second model and validated by me. The analysis scripts and the tables behind every figure are published with it on Zenodo. My messages are not, because they contain client work.

The End of Software: Why Code No Longer Matters and Relationships Are Everything

A manifesto, October 2026. Also as a PDF. It carries the DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23468806.

These days, isn't software the least valuable thing a software company owns?

I think software as we know it is coming to an end. Anyone can now build it, copy it or replace it, and whatever anyone can build stops protecting the company that sells it. What's left is whatever you can't download, which means well-structured data and genuinely complex solutions, and above all relationships with people.

I'm writing from daily experience (I built 80% of the software I use myself, and I don't know how to code), so I'll start with why code no longer protects anyone, then the gold rush that almost everyone building AI apps is reliving, and what software has to look like once an agent can do the work for you. After that come the one thing that can't be copied, the people companies are going to need, and the industries worth fighting in.

Code no longer protects anyone

The software I've built includes a program that manages the terminals where I run my AI agents, an AI-powered native Mac app that labels my email, answers it and sets up meetings, and a program that pulls data straight from Nasdaq and analyzes entire options chains, Greeks included, to find the best contracts and tell me whether they're actually worth trading. I used to pay a subscription for every one of those, and now I build them myself.

If someone like me, with no programming background, can build those tools, polish them to my liking and get them running, don't you think any company could? And what happens when every company embraces AI and nobody outsources software anymore? Plenty of companies will drop HubSpot the day 300 extra leads push its account into a higher contact tier and a bigger monthly bill.

The markets have already noticed. As I write this, in mid-October 2026, HubSpot is down 42% year to date and Monday.com is down 39%, while Shopify was down 41% at one point this spring. The selloff even has a name now, the SaaSpocalypse, which is shorthand for the fear that AI agents able to do the work on their own will make per-seat licensing obsolete.

That makes sense to me, given that there's an open-source option for almost everything and cheap AI models that answer questions and write code. Software has been losing value layer by layer: first the frontend (now very easy to build), then the backend, and finally infrastructure and operations. Now add REA (Reverse Engineer Anything), an open-source tool that lets an AI agent reverse-engineer any app installed on your computer, even one from a company worth hundreds of millions of dollars, and then build you your own version. Small businesses are already asking the obvious question: why pay hundreds of euros a month for third-party software when a tool like that exists? Code doesn't work as a moat anymore, and generic SaaS has gone down with it.

Everyone is digging for gold, and only the shovel sellers win

And yet almost everyone keeps building software expecting to sell it. I was stuck in that trap for months, seeing AI as a way to supercharge my ideas and build solutions for companies faster. All I was really doing was building traditional solutions with AI instead of accepting that we don't have to keep building the same thing.

Right now the default move is to pay $200 a month for an AI coding tool and use it to build general-purpose software that anyone can adapt. The result looks exactly like fast fashion, a market drowning in supply, full of sloppy, disposable products. I think there's a bubble in business AI apps that all look alike, with nothing innovative about them, trying to sell a solution today that would have been fantastic six years ago. Lots of people are building, very few are making it and almost nobody is selling, because when everything is this easy the only thing left is a price war. The moment a rival ships a feature, markets it better or is simply cheaper, you have to match it, and it gets so competitive that you can't grow. You stop thinking about growing and start thinking about holding on, and that's a war I won't fight.

It reminds me of the California gold rush, when people came from all over the world to strike it rich, and in the end the only ones who got rich were the ones selling shovels. Today the shovels are the AI models that write code, the data centers and the chips. Everyone knows that part. For anyone building software, though, it means that every $200 monthly subscription someone pays to build generic software and sell it is a shovel sold, and the seller gets paid every time, whether the digger strikes gold or not. And the gold, meaning generic software, is now worthless, because everyone is digging with the same shovel and pulling out the same thing. What I mean is that you shouldn't be just another sheep in the herd, building software for nobody. Or for everybody. I think it's idiotic to start a company building an agent that does everything, because an agent built to do everything ends up doing nothing well.

Why five apps and not one?

Take my own case.

I don't like Outlook, Teams or Calendar, but I need them every day, and inside them I only do a handful of things: send emails, save drafts, schedule sends and book meetings. Why would I do that in an app with 300 features? The logical move would be some version of the Pareto principle, keeping the 20% of features that handle 80% of the work. That's how most people are approaching it, and on top of that they bolt an agent onto a panel on the right. Great.

We keep working like this only because that's how it's always been done. I'm not talking about Microsoft or HubSpot in particular; I'm talking about SaaS in general. If after years on a platform you can't improve it, or you can't think of a single way to make it fit you better, you haven't thought about it enough. SaaS is built for the masses so that it works for everyone, but why should we use a system where we have to click around and spend hours managing it? Can't we just forget traditional SaaS and embrace the new era?

In that new era, the agent works inside the app itself. It can perform every action the app offers, it moves between screens, it understands context and it writes like you, because while you were building it you gave it all your emails so it could learn your style. If you can build that, why would you treat AI as an add-on rather than the core of the product? Why can't you just ask it what to do, how to do it or what to reply, instead of digging through your inbox?

The agent and the interface draw on the same data. This agent oversees the work and carries it out, and it can do anything the app can do, because it works directly on the app's data and actions rather than on buttons someone designed for everybody. You teach it and give it rules to follow. For example, I told mine that whenever it proposes two dates in an email, it should create a calendar invite for each one with "(TC)" at the start of the title, for "to confirm". That way, while I'm waiting for the client to tell me which slot works, I don't book another meeting on top of it and then have to tell them the slot is gone, which makes me look bad. You can ask it to alert you about anything at all, not just whatever a list of preset options allows, and every time you correct it, it learns. Because it knows the whole app, when you ask for something that doesn't exist yet, it puts it together from the actions it already knows, so the agent ends up building itself around you. Why fight an interface and features you don't care about when it can do that for you? I made this point in my essay "La máquina del futuro" (The Machine of the Future): the machine of the future is the tool that can take action in the real world simply from a plain-language description of what you need.

That's why I'm proposing a new way of working, where the interface stops being where you act and becomes a live view of what's happening. The agent acts, and I sit next to it, watching and correcting it as it goes. Email and the stock market aren't going anywhere in the next decade, and neither is the habit of running AI alongside them.

All these new AI companies want to take us back to the same old thing. On a typical day I already juggle four different apps, and every one of these new AI companies wants to be the fifth. And why do I need five in the first place? Why not two, or just one of my own that handles everything while I keep an eye on how it behaves and spend my time on more interesting, more creative things? We have to stop building 2010-era software and start building connected, intelligent software that lives outside conventional apps.

The only thing that can't be copied

If code doesn't protect anyone anymore, what does? A small company will build its own software with its own AI specialist. A big one, before it builds anything, needs data infrastructure across the entire company. That's why I've focused on what I think is hardest to replace: very complex solutions for very large companies. It sounds obvious, even easy to replicate, until you realize that a single solution means integrating with 15 different APIs, setting up your own databases and building flawless data pipelines so the AI doesn't hallucinate. AI writes the code for all of that too, these days. What no model can hand you is an understanding of a large company's data, or the trust it takes for that company to open its data up to you. The fact that someone who can't code built all of my own software is proof enough that code isn't the hard part anymore.

That's where the human relationship comes in, and I don't see it going anywhere. In that same essay I wrote that a company's value lies in the people who work there, and always will, however far technology advances. I think software will become increasingly custom-built, developed together with the client company, with outside partners working inside the business to solve its problems. Unless an idea is truly disruptive, I don't think generic software has much of a future, because the market for generic tools is already saturated with people and companies selling software. A company hands its data to someone it already has a relationship with. That's why I say relationships are everything: anyone can copy a vendor's code, but the relationship stays with whoever knows the client's data and problems.

So I believe the right order is to build the company's own data infrastructure first, across the whole company and with proper data governance, and only then build specific apps for the company or for one department on top of it. Whatever progress we make as a society will come from the average company having better-structured, more complete data, much more than from having more tools. Instead of trying to build better CRMs or ERPs, like everyone else, we need to build solutions that make sure a company never needs a CRM again, with specific apps for specific employees.

Who wants to hire a HubSpot expert, pay thousands of euros for a new employee and, on top of that, keep paying thousands more every month for a platform that sits on your data and locks you into its services? When the system is yours, your data can live on your own machines in your own office, and you don't have to worry about where it ends up. Isn't getting out of that one of the greatest advances in history?

Every company's Avengers

Companies will gradually stop adapting to generic software that someone else built for everyone, and they'll start building their own, because the barrier to entry is minimal. In small companies, one prompt engineer will do all of that work, and in large ones there will be one in every department, responsible for every pain point their colleagues have. They'll be the company's Avengers, the people who modernize it and do the work that actually moves it forward, and they'll put an end to SEO, to junior roles and to the high cost of paying people for repetitive tasks. That doesn't mean you stop hiring; you'll hire people for how they think and for their potential to grow inside the company, not for being better at a mechanical task, since a system trained for that can already handle it.

I know plenty of companies still resist change, or haven't been exposed to AI much, and see it as a chatbot that answers questions. They don't see everything it can do when it oversees and carries out the work of an entire department. But those same companies are swamped with day-to-day work, and they'll be increasingly open to hiring real experts who can handle their data, because their Avengers can't build anything on top of data nobody has organized. I'm just a little ahead of the curve because I use AI heavily every single day.

No pirates and no price wars

If relationships are everything, it matters a great deal who you have them with. Some industries have far more complex problems than others, and plenty are much better bets than marketing.

I know this is going to annoy people, but I see marketing agencies as pirates. They take other people's services and resell them to the end client, adding management or content on top. They don't actually make anything (the only thing they really market is themselves). They're ruthless on price, because their whole business is margins and client management. They'll fight over every last euro, and they'll always go after your margins, because that's exactly their job: negotiating on both sides of the table. I've seen this firsthand at other companies, and while not every agency is like this, most of the ones I've dealt with are loyal to no one. They set false expectations that someone else has to deliver on, they're extremely demanding and they're rarely grateful. They're masters of reselling and negotiating, but that doesn't mean you have to do business with them.

They take that pressure from their own clients and pass it down the food chain, and whoever builds software for marketing walks straight into the middle of it. With this much competition, the whole sector has turned into a battle of pirates. And trust me, you wouldn't want to be on anyone's ship.

Competition leads to mediocrity rather than success, which is why I prefer industries like healthcare, defense or transportation, where the problems are more serious, where you don't compete on price, and where you can grow as far as you need to without always having a market leader to beat. In those industries, keeping your data on your own machines, where it never leaves your hands, matters even more. As Peter Thiel put it, "Competition is for losers."

Software adapts to you, not the other way around

No more adapting to software, buying licenses and learning how to use it. If there's one thing I want people to take away from all this, it's that software isn't worth anything anymore and relationships are everything.

Founder letter, 2026

The first year of Hellomatik: what we built, what we published, what we learned, and what we bet

Madrid, September 2026. Hellomatik was incorporated on 28 July 2025, so this is the first September on which there is a year to account for. I am Isaac Correa, one of its co-founders, and I intend to write one of these every September: what we built, what we published, what we learned, and what we bet. Everything in it is either already published with a date on it or can be checked at the place the link goes to. Where it cannot be checked, I say so.

Where the year started

The company started from a habit rather than a plan. Between December 2022 and November 2024 I delivered three hundred Looker Studio dashboards for clients, one commission at a time, starting with a first sale of seven dollars on the last day of 2022. Almost nobody in those three hundred jobs asked me for a chart. They asked a question about their own business and hoped a chart would answer it. The ones who left happiest were the ones who could finally say a sentence out loud about their own company and know it was true. That is the habit: the interpretation goes next to the number, and a number nobody can read is decoration.

Something else was true in every one of those companies. The most valuable information in the building was not in any system. It was in the head of the person who reconciled things by hand every day: transport invoices against shipments, the sales figure of the month that comes out different on every screen, which orders did not leave yesterday and why. Hellomatik exists to write that down.

The first version was a system that answered restaurant phone calls: a voice on top of a database. It repeated what was in the record accurately, and when the record did not contain the answer it produced a plausible one in the same even tone. The two well-known fixes, looking things up instead of recalling them and saying “I do not know”, helped and decided nothing: they do not decide which of three disagreeing systems is believed about a given field, and they cannot look up the conditions a business applies every day and has never written anywhere. Before the software, then, there is a document. That document is what the year went into.

What we do, in four sentences

We set out how your company works: its data, its rules with their exact constants, and who may do what. We read your systems without writing to them. The agents only do what you approve: every action is declared in the map and authorised by you. And every figure we publish comes from a case measured at a customer’s site, and every case is published with what it does not show.

Those are the four sentences we repeat everywhere, on purpose, and I do not expect them to change. The language model is the last piece, not the first: models are a commodity, and one can be swapped for another in an afternoon. Data, everybody has. What almost nobody has is a living, governed model of how their company works. The model can change. The governance cannot.

What is running

The technical report states the scope of what the method runs on rather than implying it, and I repeat that scope here because it is the only part of this letter that is not an argument. At the time the report was written, three systems were running and one was open to the public, each on a different rung of the ladder from answering to watching to acting, and each with its limit published.

The system, and what it doesThe declared limit

The measured results are published case by case on Hellomatik’s site, each with what it does not show, and without naming the installation. A few of them, in the form the cases use. In an automated warehouse we measured, an order takes three minutes to pick and four and a half hours, at the median, to reach the person who picks it; nobody had measured that wait before, because it lived between two systems, and it is a measurement, not a result: it has no “after”. In the same installation, 41 per cent of the emails in the customer-service inbox were people asking where their order was; now a list of what did not ship yesterday arrives every morning with the cause, and whether that percentage falls is measured from the day the client signs the channel open. Closing a year of transport invoices, 180 a month in 30 formats, takes eight minutes, where it used to take days of a person who knows what they are doing. And 2,132 orders were held with no reason recorded anywhere, which is not a failure of the people; it is what happens when the map is in their heads and not on paper.

If we cannot name the customer, we say “an automated warehouse” or “a distributor that sells to other businesses” and keep the number. The number is the proof; the logo is not.

What we published

We chose to publish the method as a technical report with a DOI rather than as a brochure, because a technical report can be cited, checked and taken apart, and a DOI puts a fixed, dated version of the text in a repository that is not ours. The people we want reading it, operations directors, systems people, the person in a company who is quietly responsible for the ERP, read papers.

Three works carry a DOI. How we build a company’s ontology is the method itself: six layers, twelve rules the engines enforce, and one procedure that builds the model backwards from the first agents, deposited as 10.5281/zenodo.22647735. The machine of the future was written in November 2025 and published in September 2026 without changing a line, so that the date on it means something; it is where I set down what I think the next tool is and what it will always need from us, at 10.5281/zenodo.22671771. And The cliff of innovation names the point where the hard part of building something new stops being building it and becomes integrating it, and states the limit we found at the edge, at 10.5281/zenodo.22704763.

In September I put all of it in one order, in a long essay called The map of a company: nineteen sections with notes, drawn only from those three documents, the pages of this site and the sources they cite, with nothing added that is not in them. If you read one thing from this year, read that; if you read one section of it, read the one on which actions can be undone: the group of actions nothing puts back is usually the shortest list a company can write, and it is the one that decides how much a system may do.

The rest of what I have written is short and lives on this site: the first week, when two competent people give two confident answers to the same question; four questions a company can put to its own people before any software; the vocabulary, each word with what it is not; and the pieces on the people I look for, why a product is aimed at one person or at nobody, and why the tool exists before it is built. Everything published away from this site is listed in one place.

What we learned

The report records three findings that changed the procedure, and they are the most useful paragraphs in it. Language turned out to be a property of the visitor, not of each message, and is now session state with a written precedence, tested before anything is switched on. A phase that depends on people acquiring a new habit, such as copying an inbox in, is not data; it is an assumption, and it now gets written down as a risk before the work starts. And at a manufacturer selling through subsidiaries, the field that looked like the customer identified who gets invoiced, not who buys: column names lie, and their contents almost never do, which is the entire reason the second step of the procedure is to read a real sample of records before naming anything.

Two things were confirmed rather than learned. The one requirement to start is not technical: somebody inside the company has to be able to say, for each decision under discussion, what the right answer is and why, because that is what the model writes down; if that person does not exist or has no time, the project stops there, and it is better to learn it on day one than in month three. And the limit of a system that acts inside a company is not the model and not the computing power, both of which improve every month without our help. The possibilities end where the creativity of the last person who uses it ends. The product’s job is to make asking easy. The company’s job is to have people who ask.

What we will not do

A letter like this is more useful for what it rules out than for what it promises, so here is what the rules cost, said plainly. Your team does not pull a new report on its own: every business question is a function written by hand, with its formula, because a free report generator produces reports that cannot be compared with each other. There is no forecasting with trained models: the signals are explicit rules that the person responsible can argue with and change. The initial load runs at night and takes hours; an agent takes time to switch on, because it is not activated without a frozen baseline, a stop rule and a rehearsal; and the daily series reach yesterday, not now. No agent writes except through a typed action declared in advance, and no limit lives in a prompt, because a limit in a prompt is a request to the model and a limit in the data is something a person can read, audit and change without redeploying anything.

We also do not name clients, and we do not publish a price. The licence is for the whole company, with unlimited users, and the figure is agreed in the first meeting.

The bets

A description that predicts nothing is a story, so the predictions carry dates. I keep the ones I have put a date on in one place: four so far, all due in 2028, three from the working paper on the cliff and one earlier, on X, each with where it was said and how anyone can check it. Next September this letter will report on them as they stand, and when the date comes, the row on that page will say held or missed, with the original wording untouched.

The people

I do not choose people by their grades or their titles. I look for two things, and neither of them appears on a CV: someone who does not care what other people think of them, including me, and will say what they see instead of what they guess I want to hear; and initiative shown where nobody asked for it, something small built on their own, or an idea tried and not reached, with the exact place where it stopped. People with both signs are rare, and they are worth four of anyone else, not because they work four times as hard but because they need to be told once, they notice what nobody assigned, and they keep the rest of the room honest without meaning to. Being resourceful makes you a better machine. Being hungry makes you a better human.

The engineer who sits in the meeting is the engineer who writes the code, and stays. That is the whole difference between us and a consultant who delivers a report and leaves, and it is the argument a large company cannot make.

Where this goes

The ambition is to be the reference company for this category in Europe, starting in Spain and entering each country through the subsidiaries of the clients who are already there. I say that as an ambition of our own, without comparing us to anybody by name.

And the end the map serves has not changed since I wrote it down in November 2025. Running a company will stop being a manual, fragmented activity spread across many separate tools; when a task is repetitive, can be expressed as rules and is necessary for an organisation to keep going, sooner or later it gets automated. What stays human is the deciding: the value of a company is the people who work in it, and all of this works only with a human component in its decisions. A company, to me, is like a child: if you look after it, it grows, and it needs you every day. Anyone who owns a small business knows the wish to step away for a few days and come back to find the clients answered and the work done. What we are building is what lets that child grow on its own. Put without the metaphor: the difference between having a business and being tied to it.

That is the year. A voice that answered the phone became a document a company can act on; the document became a method with a DOI; the method is running, with its limits written down, in four systems; and the argument is now in one place, dated, so that it can be disagreed with in detail. Next September I will write what held.

The people I look for

My name is Isaac Correa, and I do not choose the people I work with by their grades or their titles. I look for two things, and neither of them appears on a CV. Here is what they are, and why I trust them more than the paper.

Why grades stopped being the signal

For a long time the marks and the title were a fair shortcut. They told you that someone could take in a fixed body of knowledge and reproduce it under pressure, and that used to be most of the job. Artificial intelligence now reproduces fixed knowledge better than any of us, and it does not get tired. The shortcut has not become useless, but it has lost most of what it used to measure.

What the work needs today is not intelligence in that sense. It is ingenuity: the ability to do a task that did not exist last year, with tools that did not exist last month. Nobody has a certificate for that yet, so I stopped asking for one.

The first sign: they do not care what you think of them

The first thing I look for is someone who does not care what other people think of them. That includes me, the person sitting across the table. Not rudeness; independence. Someone who says what they see instead of what they guess I want to hear, and who will keep saying it after they have joined.

That person will tell me when I am wrong. A company where nobody tells the founder he is wrong still finds out, only later and from a client. I would rather hear it on a Tuesday from someone in the room.

The second sign: something done outside the classroom

The second thing is initiative shown where nobody asked for it. Somebody who built something small on their own. Somebody who had an idea, tried to make it exist, could not, and can tell you exactly where it stopped. That last one counts as much as the first. The person who tried and did not get there has learned more about making things than the person who never left the syllabus.

What they made does not have to be good. It has to be theirs, and they have to be able to talk about it for ten minutes without notes.

Worth four

People with both signs are rare, and when I find one I have stopped pretending otherwise: they are worth four of anyone else. Not because they work four times as hard. Because they need to be told once, they notice what nobody assigned, and they keep the rest of the room honest without meaning to.

Two kinds of good

There is a line I use to explain the difference, and I mean it literally. Being resourceful makes you a better machine. Being hungry makes you a better human.

Resourceful is valuable: problems get solved, things get shipped. But a machine can be resourceful now, and it gets more resourceful every month. Hunger is the part that does not come from a manual. It is wanting the thing to exist badly enough to do the work nobody assigned. Every person I would want beside me on a hard week has the second, and the first followed on its own.

The cliff of innovation

There is a point in building something new where the hard part changes. I am Isaac Correa, co-founder of Hellomatik, and I have a name for it: the cliff of innovation. This is what it is, why we walked toward it, and what we found at the edge.

The easy road

When Hellomatik began, the easy road was clear. Take one of the tools that already existed, add our own touch, and sell it. Thousands of companies did exactly that in 2023 and 2024, and there is nothing wrong with the choice: it is fast, and the risk is known in advance.

We went the other way. Each time we tried to take a step forward and see how far a tool like ours could actually go, we got a little closer to a place I started calling the cliff.

Where the cliff is

The cliff of innovation is the point where what you want to make is either not invented yet, or has to be assembled from so many different parts that the difficulty stops being building it. The difficulty becomes integrating it: putting the parts together well enough that what you have looks like one ecosystem, and not like a Frankenstein.

That is the whole definition, and it took me a while to see how much it explains. Most of the parts exist. A model that understands speech, a model that understands language, a database, a phone line, a calendar, a mailbox. What does not exist, for any given company, is the thing that makes those parts behave like one system that knows that company: what is true, which rules it already follows, which actions can be undone, who is allowed to approve what.

Why integration is the real work

Bolting parts together is easy and it shows. You can tell a Frankenstein from across the room: every seam is visible, every part has its own idea of what a customer is, and the person using it has to translate between them in their head. An ecosystem is the opposite. The seams are inside, the parts agree about the world, and the person using it only has to say what they want.

Getting from the first to the second is where most of our year went, and it is why we published the method as a technical report with a DOI rather than as a list of features. Features are the parts. The method is the integration, written down so that somebody else can check it.

The last person

Standing at the cliff, you also find out what a tool like this is really limited by. It is not the model, and it is not the computing power; both get better every month without any help from us. I said it once without planning to, and it has stayed true since: the possibilities end where the creativity of the last person who uses it ends.

A tool that can act inside a company can only do what somebody inside that company thought to ask of it. Which leaves two jobs. The product’s job is to make asking easy. The company’s job is to have people who ask.

The long version

This page is the short version. The argument in full, with the four things that are missing in every company, the five seams of a badly integrated system, a worked example of the permission table, the scope the method is running on, three predictions dated for 2028 and the paper’s own limits, is a nine-page working paper: The cliff of innovation: why integration, not construction, is the hard part of building a system that acts inside a company (PDF, September 2026, self-published, not peer reviewed). It is deposited on Zenodo with a DOI, 10.5281/zenodo.22704763, so the file will still resolve when this site does not.
