Bets, with dates

I am Isaac Correa, co-founder of Hellomatik. A prediction without a date is an opinion, and an opinion cannot be wrong. This page keeps the predictions I have put a date on, each with where I said it and how anyone can check it when the date comes. Nothing here is edited after the fact: if a bet turns out wrong, the row will say so, with the date it was settled.

Four bets so far, all of them due in 2028. They are the ones already published in my own words elsewhere; this page only collects them.

1. From 2028 on, an ordinary computer, the one already on the desk, will run a model worth using.

Said

In full
“My date is 2028. From then on I expect an ordinary computer, the one already on the desk, to run a model worth using. Something fascinating, that a company can put its real work through without feeling it settled for less.”
Check
In 2028, take a computer bought for an office in 2026 and run a company’s real work through a model that runs on it alone. If a company would do that without feeling it settled for less, the bet held.

Why
Because the parts they share are the same parts and the touch is small.
Check

Open.

3. The durable value in this category will belong to whoever holds a company’s written map: its truth, rules, reversibility and permissions.

Open.

Open.

The people I look for

My name is Isaac Correa, and I do not choose the people I work with by their grades or their titles. I look for two things, and neither of them appears on a CV. Here is what they are, and why I trust them more than the paper.

Why grades stopped being the signal

For a long time the marks and the title were a fair shortcut. They told you that someone could take in a fixed body of knowledge and reproduce it under pressure, and that used to be most of the job. Artificial intelligence now reproduces fixed knowledge better than any of us, and it does not get tired. The shortcut has not become useless, but it has lost most of what it used to measure.

What the work needs today is not intelligence in that sense. It is ingenuity: the ability to do a task that did not exist last year, with tools that did not exist last month. Nobody has a certificate for that yet, so I stopped asking for one.

The first sign: they do not care what you think of them

The first thing I look for is someone who does not care what other people think of them. That includes me, the person sitting across the table. Not rudeness; independence. Someone who says what they see instead of what they guess I want to hear, and who will keep saying it after they have joined.

That person will tell me when I am wrong. A company where nobody tells the founder he is wrong still finds out, only later and from a client. I would rather hear it on a Tuesday from someone in the room.

The second sign: something done outside the classroom

The second thing is initiative shown where nobody asked for it. Somebody who built something small on their own. Somebody who had an idea, tried to make it exist, could not, and can tell you exactly where it stopped. That last one counts as much as the first. The person who tried and did not get there has learned more about making things than the person who never left the syllabus.

What they made does not have to be good. It has to be theirs, and they have to be able to talk about it for ten minutes without notes.

Worth four

People with both signs are rare, and when I find one I have stopped pretending otherwise: they are worth four of anyone else. Not because they work four times as hard. Because they need to be told once, they notice what nobody assigned, and they keep the rest of the room honest without meaning to.

Two kinds of good

There is a line I use to explain the difference, and I mean it literally. Being resourceful makes you a better machine. Being hungry makes you a better human.

Resourceful is valuable: problems get solved, things get shipped. But a machine can be resourceful now, and it gets more resourceful every month. Hunger is the part that does not come from a manual. It is wanting the thing to exist badly enough to do the work nobody assigned. Every person I would want beside me on a hard week has the second, and the first followed on its own.

The cliff of innovation

There is a point in building something new where the hard part changes. I am Isaac Correa, co-founder of Hellomatik, and I have a name for it: the cliff of innovation. This is what it is, why we walked toward it, and what we found at the edge.

The easy road

When Hellomatik began, the easy road was clear. Take one of the tools that already existed, add our own touch, and sell it. Thousands of companies did exactly that in 2023 and 2024, and there is nothing wrong with the choice: it is fast, and the risk is known in advance.

We went the other way. Each time we tried to take a step forward and see how far a tool like ours could actually go, we got a little closer to a place I started calling the cliff.

Where the cliff is

The cliff of innovation is the point where what you want to make is either not invented yet, or has to be assembled from so many different parts that the difficulty stops being building it. The difficulty becomes integrating it: putting the parts together well enough that what you have looks like one ecosystem, and not like a Frankenstein.

That is the whole definition, and it took me a while to see how much it explains. Most of the parts exist. A model that understands speech, a model that understands language, a database, a phone line, a calendar, a mailbox. What does not exist, for any given company, is the thing that makes those parts behave like one system that knows that company: what is true, which rules it already follows, which actions can be undone, who is allowed to approve what.

Why integration is the real work

Bolting parts together is easy and it shows. You can tell a Frankenstein from across the room: every seam is visible, every part has its own idea of what a customer is, and the person using it has to translate between them in their head. An ecosystem is the opposite. The seams are inside, the parts agree about the world, and the person using it only has to say what they want.

Getting from the first to the second is where most of our year went, and it is why we published the method as a technical report with a DOI rather than as a list of features. Features are the parts. The method is the integration, written down so that somebody else can check it.

The last person

Standing at the cliff, you also find out what a tool like this is really limited by. It is not the model, and it is not the computing power; both get better every month without any help from us. I said it once without planning to, and it has stayed true since: the possibilities end where the creativity of the last person who uses it ends.

A tool that can act inside a company can only do what somebody inside that company thought to ask of it. Which leaves two jobs. The product’s job is to make asking easy. The company’s job is to have people who ask.

The long version

This page is the short version. The argument in full, with the four things that are missing in every company, the five seams of a badly integrated system, a worked example of the permission table, the scope the method is running on, three predictions dated for 2028 and the paper’s own limits, is a nine-page working paper: The cliff of innovation: why integration, not construction, is the hard part of building a system that acts inside a company (PDF, September 2026, self-published, not peer reviewed). It is deposited on Zenodo with a DOI, 10.5281/zenodo.22704763, so the file will still resolve when this site does not.

A product is aimed at one person, or at nobody

I am Isaac Correa, and I learned this selling, not building. It is the difference between two sentences, said to the same person about the same product, and only one of them worked.

The sentence that sold nothing

Early on, when I sat down with someone who might become a client, I explained what we had built. A company that uses artificial intelligence to automate a business with its own data. Every word of that is true, and to me it is the most interesting sentence in the world.

To the person across the table it is nothing. I remember saying it to someone who sells property, and I remember the answer, because I have heard it in several voices since: I love the idea, tell me about it some day. That is what people say when you have described yourself instead of them. It is a kind answer, and it is a no.

The sentence that did

So with him I stopped, and I did not mention artificial intelligence again. I asked whether his customer management system gave him trouble. It did. I told him we had solved exactly that for several companies already, and that if he wanted, we could look at his.

That was the conversation. Same company, same product, same afternoon. The only thing that changed was the sentence: his problem, named, with proof that it had been solved before. You do not go to a client with the technology in front. You go with their problem in front, and the technology stays behind it until they ask.

One person, or nobody

The lesson underneath is short. A product is aimed at one specific person, or it is aimed at nobody. Not a segment, not a market, not “small and medium businesses”. A person with a job, a tool that gets in their way, and a Tuesday afternoon when it got in the way again.

When you can describe that person, everything else follows: what the product does first, what it refuses to do, what the first screen says, what the first sentence of the sales call is. When you cannot, you end up describing yourself, and you get told how much they love the idea.

How it shows up in the product

This is why, when we take on a company, the first thing we write down is not what the system will do. It is what the business already does: the rules it follows, the actions it takes, who approves what. The agents are then given a goal and an intention, not a chat window. They are aimed at a job that a specific person in that company already has, and their success is measured by whether that person’s afternoon got easier.

Three hundred dashboards taught me the first half of this, and I wrote that down in what selling taught me. The property seller taught me the second half in one conversation.

The tool exists before it is built

I am Isaac Correa, co-founder of Hellomatik in Madrid, and the best feeling I have had in my work is not the one people assume. Here is what it is, and the rule I took from it about when a thing is really finished.

A summer of screens

Before a single line of Hellomatik existed, there was a summer of one person thinking. I spent the first month talking through what the thing had to be: how it would be built, how it would be explained, which pages it needed, what was still missing. I spent the second month turning that conversation into a design, one screen at a time. If I had to describe the craft in a sentence: take an idea that lives in your head, put it into words, and turn the words into pictures. I have written about the order I work in, and that summer is where the order came from.

What I found months later

Months later, with the platform running and people using it every day, I went back to those screens and compared them with the real product. I expected to find that reality had rewritten most of it. It had not. The design had moved almost nothing.

That was not luck, and it was not stubbornness either. It was what happens when the idea is clear enough before the building starts. The building then becomes execution: careful, slow, but not a search.

The rule

The rule I took from it is one I now apply to everything: when you have the idea that clear, you can create the whole tool before you build it. Design is not a sketch of the product that the code will improve on. Design is the product, waiting for its code. If a screen cannot say which decision it helps somebody make, it does not survive the design, and it never has to be taken out of the code.

People sometimes read that as a reason to build faster. It is the opposite. It is a reason to think longer, because the thinking is the part that does not get redone.

The best feeling

People assume that the best moment in this work is the sale, or the applause afterwards. It is neither, and I want to be precise about it because it is the thing that keeps me at this.

The best feeling I have had in my life is seeing something that only existed inside my head working outside it, used by people who never saw the inside of my head. A screen I drew in a quiet month, and a stranger clicking on it on a busy afternoon because it does what they needed. There is nothing else in this work that comes close, and it is available to anyone who is willing to finish the thinking first.

How Hellomatik started

The first version of the idea was a system that answered restaurant phone calls. What it turned into was a way of writing down how a company decides. This page is the route between those two things, in the order it happened.

A voice

I had spent two years building Looker Studio dashboards for other people’s businesses. A dashboard shows numbers and leaves the reading to whoever opens it. The next thing to try was letting someone ask instead of read, and restaurants were the first case, because a restaurant’s phone rings all day with questions that have exact answers and nobody in the building is free to pick it up.

What you get from that is a voice on top of a database. It repeats what is in the record accurately. Ask it something the record does not contain — whether the kitchen serves late on a public holiday, whether a dish has nuts in it — and it produces a plausible answer in the same even tone it uses for the things it does know.

I shipped it before I had any way of telling its right answers from its wrong ones, which is a bad order to do things in and the reason the rest of this page exists.

The cheap fixes, and where they stop

There are two well-known answers to that. Let the system look things up instead of recalling them, so the answer comes from the record rather than the model. And let it say it does not know instead of filling the gap. I used both. They reduce how often it invents, and they are worth doing, but neither one decides anything.

The first thing they do not decide is which record. A business with a booking system, a point of sale and a spreadsheet has three answers to “is that table free”, and they disagree. Looking it up is only a procedure once somebody has decided, in writing, which of the three is believed about that particular field, and in the businesses I sat with that decision was not the same for every field.

The second is that a good part of what the phone gets asked is not in any record at all. Can this table be held for twenty minutes without a card. Can that customer order again with an invoice still unpaid. Can the party of twelve book on a Friday. Those have exact answers, the business applies them consistently, and they are not written anywhere — they are conditions somebody decided once and everyone learned. Looking them up is not possible, because there is nothing to look up.

None of this is new. Deciding which system is authoritative for which data is an old discipline with its own literature, its own vendors and its own well-documented ways of failing: master data management, data governance, more recently data contracts. Those programmes have driven system-to-system integration for years, not only human reporting, so the machine-readable part is not the new bit either. What was different in my case was the size of company. That work is normally bought by organisations large enough to run a programme around it, and in the small and mid-size businesses I sat with the same decisions existed nowhere at all — not in a tool, not in a document. They were held by whoever had been there longest.

A brain

So before the software there is a document: which system is authoritative for each thing, what conditions the business already applies, which exceptions are live and when they expire, and who may approve what. Not a summary of the business, but the specific decisions it makes without noticing it is making them.

One piece of it — two departments settling what they each mean by “customer” — takes an afternoon in a room, and in my experience it does not end with a clean answer. Both definitions are usually right about their own department.

A company has hundreds of terms like that, so the honest version is that the document is never finished. What the first weeks produce is not the whole company written down: it is the part needed by whatever is being automated first, which is a much smaller thing and is the only reason the work is startable at all. The rest stays where it was, and gets written when something needs to act on it. Anyone who tells you the whole map takes a few weeks is quoting the afternoon and hoping you multiply badly.

That cost falls on the company rather than on me: it puts people in a meeting arguing about something they had been quietly disagreeing about for years, and the count of live exceptions comes out higher than expected. It also does not settle who is allowed to change the rule once it is written, which is the failure mode the older discipline is full of and which I do not consider solved.

Hands

Answering correctly and being allowed to act are separate problems, and the second one is the reason the document has to exist rather than be a good habit.

A system that answers can be wrong and cost somebody a morning. A system that sends the quote, releases the order or raises the credit hold can be wrong in a way that cannot be taken back. So before software is allowed to act, somebody has to have written down what it may do, which of those things can be undone, and who has to approve the rest. That list is part of the same few weeks, and in my experience it is the part people have thought about least before starting.

It also decides where the limit lives. A limit written into a prompt is a request to the model. A limit written as data is not self-enforcing either — something still has to check the row before acting, and that check is code somebody has to write. What changes is that the limit can be read by someone who did not write the prompt, audited after the fact, and changed without redeploying.

What the document turned out to be

By this point the document had four parts. I did not arrive at them by design; each is what was left over from a problem further up this page, and I am laying them out in that order so the derivation can be checked rather than taken.

The voice needed to know which system is believed about each thing, so the first part is the data: what exists in the business, and which record is authoritative for each field of it. The questions with no record behind them — the twenty-minute hold, the party of twelve, the customer with an invoice outstanding — needed the conditions the business already applies, written down with their exceptions and the dates those stop being true, so the second part is the rules. Letting it act needed the list of what it may actually do and which of those things can be undone, so the third part is the actions. And because some of those cannot be undone, the fourth part is who may approve what.

Data, rules, actions, permissions. The word for that is ontology, used the way information systems have used it for decades rather than the way philosophy does — I define it, and what it is not, on another page. It is an unfashionable term and I keep it because the alternatives mislead: “knowledge base” suggests pages for a human to read, and “configuration” suggests settings for a program that already exists. This is neither. It is a company stating its own decisions in a form that something other than a person can act on.

The version I ended up with is written out in full in the report, called “How we build a company’s ontology”. What it mostly contains is the order to ask things in and the mistakes that make you redo the first week.

What it looks like at production size

The method is published in full as a technical report with a DOI. It declares its limits, and the figures there are the closest thing to a size on this page. Four systems in production at the time of writing; the report describes two of them in any detail. One is B2B, recalculating 29,000 customers every night over 14 million sales lines. The other is logistics: 24 rules applied against live ERP and warehouse data, across 30 million operations and 460,000 locations.

Those are counts of what the systems run over, not of what they improved, and the report is my own deposit rather than an audit by anyone else. So they are a description of size and nothing more.

The checkable part

Very little of what I have written above can be verified from outside. Hellomatik’s first client was a large clinic, and I am not naming it or its city, so that is not a case study and I am not going to present it as one — it is a fact about my working life that you would have to take on trust, which is the same footing as most of this page. The report is deposited with a DOI, which is an address rather than a review: it fixes the argument in one place with a date on it so it can be disagreed with in detail.

The company is a matter of public record. Hellomatik was incorporated in Madrid on 28 July 2025. My appointment as joint administrator went into the Spanish commercial register and was published in the BORME on 4 August 2025, sheet M-861159, with the Agencia Estatal BOE as the source. That proves a company exists and when — nothing about whether any of the above works.

Before it existed there were two months of screens, one panel at a time, each one kept only if I could say which decision it was there to support. The order I work in is on another page.

The Looker Studio years

The first thing anybody ever paid me for was an analytics dashboard, on the last day of 2022, on a freelance platform. Seven dollars. The two years that followed were Looker Studio, one commission at a time. This page is about the work itself rather than the arc it belongs to: what a job actually consisted of, what turned out to be the same in all of them, and what came out the other side.

What a commission actually was

Somebody gives you access to their GA4 property, their Search Console, sometimes their Google Ads. They send a paragraph describing what they want. The paragraph almost never describes a chart. It describes a worry: they think they are paying too much for something, or they cannot tell which half of the traffic is worth anything, or a number went down last month and nobody in the company can say why.

The job is to turn that worry into something a screen can answer, and then to build the screen. The building is the short part. Most of the time went into reading the paragraph correctly, and when I got that wrong the report was opened once and never again.

Some of those clients were one person with a shop. Some were companies of considerable size with a marketing team and an agency already in place. The worry arrived in much the same form from both.

What repeated

By the end there were three hundred of these, for clients in the United States, across Europe, in South America and in Korea. I was studying at the same time, so the meetings went in the afternoons and the deliveries went out in hours.

Three hundred businesses, and the questions their data got asked came down to a handful: which channels bring the visits, what those visits cost, which of them turn into something, and what moved since last month. The dashboards looked different from each other because the businesses did. Underneath, they were answering the same four things.

Every single one of those three hundred started from an empty canvas. That is what Looker Studio gives you, and for a tool it is the right default. It also meant rebuilding the same skeleton three hundred times before getting to the part that was actually about that client.

The reviews

A hundred and three clients left a verified review, and they are published in full on Kodalogic’s reviews page rather than summarised here. A delivery count only records that I turned up. The reviews are the part I did not write.

Kodalogic, September 2024

Kodalogic exists because of that. I founded it in Madrid in September 2024, so that the skeleton would only have to be built once.

The catalogue is six finished dashboards rather than three hundred, and it took the three hundred to see why. Six covers GA4, Search Console and Google Ads, which is where the questions above actually live. Anything past that is a variation on one of the six, and a client who needs one can make it in an afternoon from a dashboard that already works, which is not true of an empty canvas.

The other half of the decision was the interpretation. Each chart in those dashboards has the reading written next to it, in words, saying what it means when the line does what it is doing. Without that, somebody who knows the tool has to be standing next to the client for the chart to be worth anything, and nobody is paying for that person to be on call.

The fifty-eight posts

Kodalogic sells through search results, so I wrote fifty-eight posts on Looker Studio reporting for its blog, all signed and all still up. Not pieces about the company: the actual answers to the questions people were typing into Google, written out in full.

What was underneath

By the three hundredth dashboard the pattern was hard to miss, and it was not about reports. In almost every business I worked with, the data, the rules and the decisions had never been written down anywhere; they lived in people’s heads and in the habits of whoever had been there longest. Every tool that arrived had to be told the same things from scratch, and every dashboard was a fresh attempt to reconstruct them. That layer is what the company I run now works on.

Which actions can be undone

Before an action is handed to software, somebody has to be able to say how it would be taken back, and by whom. This is the list I ask a company to write, and what goes in each column.

Sort the actions by how long you have to change your mind

Not by how important they are. Two actions can matter exactly the same amount to the business and still need completely different amounts of supervision, because one of them can be put back all afternoon and the other cannot be put back at all.

Some actions are undoable. A draft is saved, a status is corrected, a note is added to a record. If it is wrong, somebody sets it back and the day carries on.

Some are expensive to undo. Stock is committed, a slot in a delivery round is taken, a credit hold is lifted. It can be reversed, but reversing it costs a phone call, or somebody’s afternoon, or a customer noticing.

And some cannot be undone. A message reaches a customer, money moves, a document goes to someone outside the company. Nothing puts those back.

The third group is usually the shortest of the three, which is why writing it down is an afternoon rather than a project.

Four columns

What it touches

The record, or the thing in the world, that ends up different. If the answer is the name of a system rather than a thing, it is not specific enough yet.

How it is undone

The exact step somebody would take. The word manually is not an answer; it means nobody has checked whether the step exists.

Who can undo it

A role, or a name. It also has to be somebody who is actually there: an action whose only recovery runs through one person is a different risk in August than it is in March.

How long the window stays open

The point after which undoing it stops being free. This is the column that people skip, and it is the one that changes the character of the others.

A filled-in row looks like this

Take releasing an order that is on credit hold. What it touches: the hold flag on that order, and the picking queue it drops into. How it is undone: put the hold back. Who can undo it: whoever is covering the credit desk that day. The window: until the picking list prints, because after that the order is physically being assembled and putting the hold back means walking into the warehouse.

Four short answers, and the last one is the reason this action can be automated at all: there is a clean point where the cost changes, and it can be written down.

The rows in the third group are shorter and less comfortable. Sending a customer the confirmation that their order has shipped: what it touches: their inbox. How it is undone: it is not. Who can undo it: nobody. The window: there is none. Nothing about that row is difficult to fill in. What is difficult is deciding, once it is written down in front of you, whether anything should be allowed to send it without a person looking.

What the finished list is for

Two things. It tells you which actions can run without anybody watching, because the worst case is a correction someone can make. And for everything else, it tells you what has to be true before it runs: who approves it, and how long they have to change their mind.

That second part is why the approval belongs in the same table as the action, with the name and the window as fields — a permission rather than a screen. An approval that lives only in a screen cannot be queried afterwards. What it leaves behind is the order in which things were clicked.

If you write nothing else, write the third group: the actions nothing puts back. It is short, and it is the only one where being wrong cannot be corrected afterwards.

What selling taught me that building never did

My first sale was seven dollars, on the last day of 2022. Everything I run today starts there, and most of what I know about products I learned on calls, not at a desk.

Seven dollars is a real number

People treat a first sale as a formality. It is not. Seven dollars means one stranger, somewhere, decided that a thing I made was worth more than the money in their pocket. Nothing I had built before had ever been tested that way. Every product opinion I hold now traces back to that test, and to the two years of repeating it.

I sold while I studied

For the next two years I sold while I studied. Between December 2022 and November 2024 I delivered three hundred Looker Studio dashboards for clients, one commission at a time, and collected 103 verified client reviews. The clients were in the United States, in Europe, in South America and in Korea. Small businesses, and companies of considerable size.

At the busiest, five meetings a day. Not five calls: five conversations where somebody explains their business to you and you have to understand it well enough to be useful by the end of the hour.

The lesson

Here is the part I would tell anyone starting. In three hundred jobs, almost nobody asked me for a chart. They asked me a question about their own business and hoped a chart would answer it. The ones who left happiest were not the ones with the prettiest dashboards. They were the ones who could finally say a sentence out loud about their own company and know it was true.

That is why the interpretation goes next to the chart. A number nobody can read is not information, it is decoration, and I had to sell three hundred times before I stopped confusing the two.

The prices went up

The prices went up as the work got better. I am not going to put figures on that, because the figures are not the point and they would be the only thing anyone remembered. The point is the direction, and the reason for it: I stopped selling a file and started selling the answer.

What it turned into

Out of those three hundred commissions came Kodalogic, founded in Madrid in September 2024: six finished dashboards, distilled from what repeated in all of them, with the interpretation written next to each chart. And out of the habit of asking what a business actually needed to know came Hellomatik, founded on 28 July 2025, and the method we published with a DOI.
